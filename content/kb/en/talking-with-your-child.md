Children usually cope better when they hear the truth in words they can understand. You don't need perfect words. You need honest, simple ones, said with love, more than once.

## Before you talk

- Ask your child's care team how to explain the treatment in words that fit your child's age. They can tell you what is true for your child.
- Many hospitals have a child life specialist. Their job is to help children understand what is happening and feel less afraid. Ask if your hospital has one.
- Decide who will be there. Many families talk together, with both parents or another trusted adult.

## Ideas by age

**Young children** often worry most about being apart from you, and about pain.

- Use short, simple sentences and the real names for things.
- Tell them it is not their fault. Young children sometimes think they caused it.
- Keep routines where you can: the same bedtime story, the same goodnight words.

**School-age children** want to know what will happen and why.

- Explain what the next few days will look like.
- Invite questions, and come back to the talk later.

**Teens** want to be included and respected.

- Include them in conversations with the care team when you can.
- Respect their privacy and their wish to have some control.
- Let them choose who they tell, and how.

## Things that help at any age

- **Don't promise what you can't know.** Instead of "it won't hurt", try "if it hurts, tell me, and we'll tell the nurse".
- **It's okay to say "I don't know".** You can add: "Let's ask the care team together."
- **Let feelings be okay.** Anger, fear, silence and jokes are all normal ways to cope.
- **Keep talking.** One big talk is less helpful than many small ones.

## Who to ask

Your child's care team for what to say about their treatment. A child life specialist, if your hospital has one, for how to say it. The hospital social worker if you're worried about how your child is coping.
